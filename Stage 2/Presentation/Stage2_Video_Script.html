Slide 1 · Title

All four on camera in front of the skid. M speaks.

Hello, we are team team name. I'm M, from Mechanical, and with me are E1, E2 and E3 from Electrical. In the next ten minutes we'll show you our automated liquid-flow skid: how it is plumbed, wired and controlled, and both operating cases running live.

Slide 2 · Set a flow, measure it, protect it, flush it

In a semiconductor fab, process fluids must arrive at a defined flow, or the chemistry on the wafer is wrong. Our skid does that job on a tabletop. Case 1 circulates process fluid from a ten-litre tank through a sump and back, measures and totalises the return flow, and protects the sump when extra fluid is suddenly dumped in. Case 2 isolates the process tank, flushes the lines from a 4.2-litre flush tank with all three pumps running, and is switched off with the button once the flush water runs out.

Slide 3 · Case 1 P&ID

This is our Case 1 P&ID in ISA symbols. The blue line is the active path, and the arrows give its direction. From the process tank, TK-01, water flows through the maintenance valve BV-03, the solenoid valve SOV-01 and the tee, into transfer pump P-01 at fifty percent, and up into the sump, TK-02. Return pump P-02, at full duty, draws from the bottom of the sump and pushes the water through the throttle valve BV-01 and the flow transmitter FT-01, back into TK-01.

Slide 4 · Every component, in flow order

Cut to the camera on the real skid. M points at each part as it is named. Keep the slide as a corner overlay if your editor allows.

Here it is on the real skid. This is the process tank, TK-01. Water leaves at the bottom, through BV-03, which stays open, through the solenoid valve, into the tee, and up into P-01. P-01 fills the sump here, and this float switch, LSH-01, detects high level. From the bottom of the sump the water goes into P-02, through BV-01, up through FT-01, and back into the top of TK-01. Slide 5 · Built for the tube we actually have

One mechanical detail changed our numbers. The paper gives an eight-millimetre pipe, but the kit tube is eight millimetres outside and five inside, and we measured 5. At three litres per minute that is 2.55 metres per second instead of about one, with roughly nine times the friction, which is why our loop gives about 2 to 2.5 litres per minute, not the pump's rated 6.1. Every tank port is a brass bulkhead with washers on both sides, every thread is Teflon-taped, we leak-tested before any electronics came near, and we never close BV-01 while P-02 runs. Over to E1.

Slide 6 · One 12 V supply, one ESP32, three drivers

Camera on the PCB and drivers, then back to the slide.

Thanks, M. Everything runs from a twelve-volt, 350-watt SMPS feeding the custom PCB. In Case 2, three pumps and the solenoid draw about eleven amps, well inside the supply's 29-amp rating. The ESP32 on the PCB is the controller. Each pump has its own BTS7960 H-bridge driver on headers U2, U3 and U4, taking PWM and enable signals from the ESP32 and power from outputs P1, P2 and P3. The solenoid valve is switched through a D4184 MOSFET module with a flyback diode across the coil. Three signals come back: the float switch on GPIO 35, the button on GPIO 34, and the flow sensor's pulses on GPIO 14.

Slide 7 · Three faults, one symptom

Three faults taught us the most, and they all looked the same: nothing moved. First, pump 2's driver gave zero volts. Its R-PWM trace is missing on the PCB, as the organisers confirmed, so we drive it from the L-PWM channel, tie the unused input high, and invert it in firmware. Second, FT-01 read exactly zero. A raw-edge counter showed the ESP32 never saw a pulse, because the sensor's red and black leads were swapped. Red to V, black to G, yellow to S fixed it. It also under-read when mounted flat, because it wasn't running full, so it now sits vertically with the flow going up. Third, the start button was ignored, though shorting its terminals worked, so we rewired it across the diagonal legs. Same symptom, three root causes, which is why fault diagnosis needs more than one signal. Over to E2.

Slide 8 · The button steps through every case

Thanks, E1. Our firmware is a state machine. In IDLE everything is off and the solenoid is closed, so a reset or power cut always lands in a safe state. One press of the button enters Case 1: the solenoid opens, P-01 ramps to fifty percent and P-02 to a hundred, with a soft start. If the float reports high level for 300 milliseconds, P-01 stops while P-02 keeps draining, and P-01 restarts only after the float has been clear for three seconds, so a sloshing surface can't make it chatter. The same button then steps the sequence on, whatever time is left: the second press starts the flush, and the third stops everything. If nobody presses, Case 1 still hands over by itself after 150 seconds.

Slide 9 · From pulses to litres

FT-01 is a turbine sensor with a Hall-effect pickup. Every pulse fires an interrupt, and pulses closer than 300 microseconds are rejected as noise. Every half second we compute frequency as pulses over time, convert it with our calibration, Q equals a f plus b, and add it up into the total litres returned. Our Python logger plots it live in litres per minute and millilitres per second.

Slide 12 · We calibrated FT-01 instead of trusting it

To trust these numbers, we calibrated FT-01 ourselves: five timed trials, collecting the return flow and counting pulses. The least-squares fit gives Q equals a f plus b, with R-squared __, maximum error __ percent and average error __ percent. In our five-millimetre tube, theory gives 0.85, 2.55 and 5.09 metres per second at one, three and six litres per minute, all turbulent. Our loop tops out near 2 to 2.5 litres per minute, so six is out of reach with this tubing, valve and sensor in the line.

Slide 13 · A ball valve is a coarse throttle

Stepping BV-01 through 25, 50, 75 and 100 percent showed a quick-opening curve: most of the flow arrives in the first half of the turn, and near full open the pump and tubing set the flow, not the valve.
